Window crank not working? Repair it before you replace the window

A casement window crank that spins, sticks or won't close is almost always a worn operator or hinge. How to tell what's wrong and why a repair beats a new window.

Casement and awning windows open with a crank. When the crank spins without moving the window, takes a lot of force, or the window won't close tight, most people assume they need a new window. Almost always, they don't.

What's actually broken

  • Handle spins, window doesn't move: stripped gears inside the crank operator.
  • Handle is loose on its shaft: a worn handle. The cheapest fix of all.
  • Window rubs or sags: bent or worn hinges.
  • Window closes but won't lock tight: the lock is out of line with its keepers, usually because the sash has sagged.

Why cranks fail

Cold weather is hard on window hardware. Forcing a window that's frozen shut strips the gears, and rust and dirt seize the mechanism. After 15 to 20 years of daily use, operators simply wear out.

The fix

Replacing a crank operator usually takes under an hour. We identify the hardware, often made by AmesburyTruth, Roto, Maxim or Encore, fit a matching or compatible part, and check the hinges and lock while we're there. See window crank repair and hinge repair.

Keep it working

  1. Spray hinges and moving parts with silicone once a year.
  2. Vacuum dirt out of the hinge tracks in spring.
  3. Never force a frozen window. Let it thaw.
